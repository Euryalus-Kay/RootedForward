This tour is live at `/tours/chicago/hyde-park` with placeholder footage so it plays end to end. Everything below is what you film and swap in. The archival stills are real public-domain images and stay. The narration is a scratch machine voice you replace with a recorded read of the same script.

## Hyde Park, the Ground That Keeps Moving  (Orientation)

**Host on camera (say this):** I’m standing in Hyde Park, on the South Side, about seven miles south of downtown. Look at these greystones. They feel like they were here before anybody, and like they’ll outlast all of us. But this block looks the way it does because powerful institutions kept deciding it should, and plenty of people got moved so it could happen. This is some of the most reshaped ground in Chicago, and the people doing the reshaping had money and a plan, and almost none of them lived here. So here is the question I want us to carry the whole way through. Who keeps deciding what this neighborhood becomes, and what happens to the people already here when they do.

**Voiceover script to record:**

> On the South Side of Chicago, about seven miles from downtown, a neighborhood called Hyde Park runs right up to Lake Michigan. Its greystones and Gothic towers look permanent, as if they had always stood here. Almost none of it was here a hundred and seventy years ago. Since then, Hyde Park has been built, cleared, and rebuilt, again and again, by institutions with money and a plan. A railroad reached the prairie first. Then a university, then a World's Fair. In the 1950s, a federal urban renewal program pushed more than fifteen thousand people off their blocks. Most of them were never asked. This is the story of who keeps deciding what this neighborhood becomes, and what happens to the people already living here.

## The Ground Before  (Before 1853)

Scratch VO length about 63.4s. clipId `vo-land`.

**Voiceover script to record:**

> Before any of this, the ground was prairie and marsh, sloping down to the lake. It was Potawatomi land, part of the Council of Three Fires, who had lived and traded along these shores for generations. They did not fight the Black Hawk War of 1832, but in its wake the United States pressed the Three Fires nations into the Treaty of Chicago, in 1833. With one signing they gave up some five million acres, this lakefront among them, and within a few years they were marched west. Around 1835 a settler named Nathan Watson raised a tavern near what is now 53rd Street and the water, on the old trail the Potawatomi had walked. The first taking set the pattern. This ground would be claimed, cleared, and remade, again and again, by whoever held the deed and the plan. In 1853 a young lawyer named Paul Cornell bought three hundred acres from Watson's widow.

## Paul Cornell's Lakefront Bet  (1853 to 1889)

Scratch VO length about 62.9s. clipId `vo-formation`.

**Voiceover script to record:**

> He named it Hyde Park, after the park in London, and sold it as the one thing a crowded city could not offer, room to breathe. The land was useless if you could not get there, so Cornell handed the Illinois Central Railroad sixty acres, and the railroad gave him a station at 53rd Street and six trains a day downtown. Prairie turned into suburb. In 1857 he put up a four-story wood hotel by the water, the Hyde Park House. Mary Todd Lincoln stayed there in 1865 with her sons Robert and Tad, a few months after her husband was killed. By 1889 the township held about eighty-five thousand people, and that year they voted to join Chicago for the water and the sewers. From the start, Cornell sold the place as selective. Near the lake, in the north, a house might cost seven thousand dollars, against two thousand to the south. The sorting of Hyde Park had already begun.

B-roll of Oak Woods Cemetery for Cornell's grave. No talking-head needed for this history chapter; let footage and archival stills carry it.

## Rockefeller's University Rises  (1890 to 1910)

Scratch VO length about 67.3s. clipId `vo-university`.

**Voiceover script to record:**

> This was not the first University of Chicago. An earlier one opened in the 1850s on land from Senator Stephen Douglas, and went bankrupt in 1886. The second began with one man's money. In 1889, John D. Rockefeller pledged six hundred thousand dollars for a new university in Chicago, on one condition, that other donors match four hundred thousand more. They did, and it incorporated in 1890. Marshall Field gave the first ten acres in Hyde Park. Rockefeller picked William Rainey Harper to run it, and Henry Ives Cobb laid out the gray Gothic quads you still walk through. Classes opened in 1892. By 1901, as other Chicago neighborhoods began to slip, Hyde Park held its value, protected, people said, by the parks, the lake, and the university. That word, protected, would soon come to mean something darker. The same institution that built these quads would help decide who got to live around them.

**Shot notes:** Film present-day Hyde Park main quadrangles in the gray limestone Gothic, with corbels, crenellations, gargoyles, and grotesques in frame. Capture Cobb Lecture Hall exterior (the first building completed, ready for the October 1892 opening). 360 capture from the center of the main quad looking across the interlocking quadrangles toward the towers. Slow walking shots along the cloistered walks to evoke the Oxford and Cambridge model. B-roll of period-style markers or plaques naming Harper, Field, and the founding dates if visible on campus. Optional archival-style stills under the host of Rockefeller, Harper, Marshall Field, and Henry Ives Cobb's campus plan. A separate establishing shot toward the lakefront ties the campus to the 1893 Exposition site nearby. Keep any generated or test imagery labeled.

## The White City in Jackson Park  (1893)

Scratch VO length about 55.5s. clipId `vo-worlds-fair`.

**Voiceover script to record:**

> For six months in 1893, Jackson Park was not a park. It was a fair. The World's Columbian Exposition opened that May, built to mark four hundred years since Columbus reached the Americas. Daniel Burnham ran the construction. Frederick Law Olmsted turned the marshy lakefront into lagoons. At the center stood the Court of Honor, white plaster halls lit at night by electricity. People called it the White City. About twenty-seven million people came. Almost none of it was built to last. To move the crowds, the Illinois Central lifted its tracks onto an embankment above the streets. That wall of earth split the neighborhood in two, and the blocks toward the lake became a place apart, Hyde Park East. The fair made this lakefront valuable ground. The fight over who it belonged to was just ahead.

For the Midway Plaisance, a tracking shot down the mile-long green strip from Jackson Park toward Washington Park. Optional talking-head beat from a local historian standing near the MSI entrance noting that this single brick-substructure building is what outlasted the fair.

## The Color Line Comes to Hyde Park  (1908 to 1948)

Scratch VO length about 85.3s. clipId `vo-color-line`.

**Voiceover script to record:**

> It started with a club. In 1908, white homeowners formed the Hyde Park Improvement Protective Club, three hundred and fifty strong, with a single aim, to remove every Black resident and worker from the neighborhood. They pledged to fire their Black servants, to boycott any agent who sold to a Black family, and to buy out the Black households who had lived here for years. One of them, Fannie Barrier Williams, a longtime resident, refused to leave. When pressure was not enough, they turned to the deed. A racially restrictive covenant was a line written into the title, barring sale or rent to anyone who was not white. Neighbors signed in blocs, so a single clause could seal a whole block. And the University of Chicago was not a bystander. Between 1933 and 1947 it spent more than eighty-three thousand dollars defending the covenants around its campus. Black Chicago kept growing the whole time, from about forty thousand people in 1910 to two hundred seventy-eight thousand by 1940. Most were pressed into a narrow corridor called the Black Belt, a few blocks wide and miles long. Inside it families paid more for less, carved into kitchenette apartments, because the color line left them nowhere else to go.

**Shot notes:** Open on present-day Hyde Park and Kenwood residential streets, slow tracking past brick three-flats and graystones that would have carried covenant clauses. Capture a 360 node at a Woodlawn corner near 6140 South Rhodes Avenue (Washington Park subdivision), exterior only, to anchor the Hansberry case. Film the University of Chicago campus edge where it meets the surrounding neighborhoods to underscore the institutional role. B-roll suggestions: archival-style inserts of a deed page and Great Migration rail imagery if licensed, otherwise hold on present-day storefronts and the 53rd Street corridor. No talking-head beat for this history chapter; narration carries it. End on a quiet exterior of a Kenwood block to hand off to the urban renewal chapter.

## How Hyde Park Drew the Lines  (1930s to 1948)

Scratch VO length about 57.5s. clipId `vo-redlining`.

**Voiceover script to record:**

> The belief that a Black family lowered the value of a block was not only prejudice. It was given the authority of science, right here. A University of Chicago economist named Homer Hoyt ranked races and nationalities by their supposed effect on land values. Then he carried that logic to Washington, as the federal government's chief land economist, where it was drawn onto maps. Block by block, neighborhoods with Black residents were outlined and marked hazardous, then cut off from loans. The country would come to call it redlining, and it spread to nearly every major city in America. An idea built in a Hyde Park classroom helped wall off the whole country. Not until 1948 did the Supreme Court take the covenants' force away, in Shelley versus Kraemer. The clauses stayed in the deeds. The courts just could not enforce them anymore.

## The University Rebuilds the Neighborhood  (1952 to 1970s)

Scratch VO length about 84.2s. clipId `vo-urban-renewal`.

**Voiceover script to record:**

> By the early 1950s the covenants were gone, and Black families were moving into the blocks around the university. Afraid its campus would be stranded in a changing neighborhood, the university set up the South East Chicago Commission in 1952 to remake those blocks. Chancellor Lawrence Kimpton chaired it, and a lawyer named Julian Levi ran it day to day. The plan they backed covered eight hundred fifty-six acres. It marked six hundred thirty-eight buildings to come down, judged too crowded or too run down to keep, many of them the older apartments where the newest Black arrivals had found rooms. The first one fell on May tenth, 1955, before the plan was even approved. The City Council made it official in 1958. All told, the plan would push out about four thousand families. The university spent twenty-nine million dollars of its own money to buy the land and rebuild it. The people who came back weren't a random slice. In those first projects, forty-six percent of the white families who lost homes stayed in Hyde Park. Among Black families, it was seventeen. Across the renewal, the neighborhood's Black population fell by about forty percent. Then the university looked south, toward Woodlawn.

## Hyde Park Now  (Today)

Scratch VO length about 111.2s. clipId `vo-present`.

**Host on camera (say this):** I'm standing in Jackson Park, and behind me is the Obama Presidential Center. People waited more than a decade for this to become reality, and for many, it's a major investment in the South Side. But there's another side of the story that didn't make it into the ribbon-cutting. As the Center was being built, Woodlawn, the neighborhood that worked for years to bring it here, became far more expensive. On the blocks closest to the project, home prices doubled. So I keep coming back to one question. When investment transforms a neighborhood, who gets to stay and be part of what they helped create?

**Voiceover script to record:**

> The Obama Presidential Center opened on June 19, 2026. Juneteenth. The museum tower runs about two hundred twenty-five feet over Jackson Park. Olmsted and Vaux laid out this park in 1871, and it's the same ground that held the 1893 World's Fair. The campus cost about eight hundred fifty million dollars. No presidential center has ever cost more. Getting it built took years. The site was picked in 2016. A group called Protect Our Parks sued over and over to stop it. Every suit failed. Construction started in 2021. And while the Center went up, Woodlawn got more expensive. On the blocks closest to it, home prices doubled after 2019. By 2024, less than a third of the housing nearby was still affordable. The pattern that began with the covenants never really broke. When Black families finally bought here, and across the South and West Sides, many bought on contract, at markups that stripped an estimated three to four billion dollars from Black Chicago. Money that should have become wealth, handed down, simply vanished. You can still measure the hole it left. Today the median white family in America holds about two hundred eighty-five thousand dollars. The median Black family holds about forty-five thousand. A gap of more than six to one. It is a pattern the neighborhood has run before. An institution arrives, the ground around it gains value, and the people already there are left to wonder if the new Hyde Park has room for them. A hundred and seventy years after Paul Cornell bought these blocks, the question underneath it all, who this ground is for, still is not settled.

**Shot notes:** Present-day exteriors of the Obama Presidential Center museum tower from multiple angles in Jackson Park, including a low upward shot to convey the ~225-foot height. Wide lakefront establishing shot showing the campus against Lake Michigan. 360 capture from the park lawn near the tower base for the immersive viewer. Host talking-head walk-and-talk on a Jackson Park path with the Center visible over the shoulder. B-roll along East Woodlawn residential streets (single-family blocks nearest the Center) showing newer renovations beside older housing; no recognizable faces without release. Optional archival still of the 1893 World's Columbian Exposition for the historic overlay. Capture exterior signage and the public entrance for the opening-date callout.
