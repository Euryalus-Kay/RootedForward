Contract Sales and Urban Renewal
The Supreme Court ended covenant enforcement in 1948. Banks still refused Black borrowers, speculators resold houses at nearly double the price on contract, and in 1958 the city approved a clearance plan that displaced about 4,000 families.

In 1937 Carl Hansberry bought a three-flat at 6140 South Rhodes, inside a covenant the university had helped defend. A mob gathered, and a chunk of concrete nearly struck his daughter Lorraine, age seven. The family won Hansberry v. Lee at the Supreme Court in 1940, Shelley v. Kraemer ended enforcement everywhere in 1948, and Lorraine Hansberry put the mob outside the window into A Raisin in the Sun.

Contract sales

Banks would not follow the ruling, so speculators filled the gap, reselling to Black families on installment contracts at prices they set themselves. If a family missed one payment, the seller kept the house and every dollar paid.

Urban renewal

The university's South East Chicago Commission drove the Hyde Park-Kenwood plan, approved November 7, 1958, the first large federally supported urban renewal program in the country. Displaced businesses got nothing, and most never reopened.
