Who Could Live in Hyde Park
The resort · 1853 to 1856

Paul Cornell, a young Chicago lawyer, bought sixty lakefront acres of farm and tavern land in August 1853 and kept buying until he held about 300 acres between 51st and 55th Streets. He named the area Hyde Park to give it a refined image and sold it as an escape from a city that drained its sewage into the lake. In 1856 he gave the Illinois Central sixty acres for a station at 53rd Street and a guarantee of daily trains. The families he recruited were wealthy and white.

The parks and Cornell's stake · 1866 to 1871

Cornell then led the campaign for public parks beside his own land. The campaign was run by landowners whose property the parks would raise in value, and Cornell held 300 acres beside the ground he was asking the state to improve. The law passed in 1869, and the commissioners bought 1,055 acres of sandbar and marsh and hired Frederick Law Olmsted and Calvert Vaux. Their 1871 plan showed lagoons, a canal down the Midway, and thirty miles of paths. The Great Fire burned the drawings that October, and Jackson Park sat mostly unfinished for twenty years.

The fair · 1893

Chicago won the World's Fair over New York, Washington, and St. Louis, and Daniel Burnham placed it on the unfinished park. Crews raised the White City in about two years, steel frames covered in white plaster. It drew about 27 million visits in a country of 65 million people, and nearby land values began rising as soon as the site was announced.

Shut out at the gates · 1893

Black Americans were shut out of the fair's planning, its exhibit halls, and nearly all of its jobs. Organizers offered one Colored American Day late in the summer. Frederick Douglass worked the season from the Haiti Pavilion, the one building that presented Black achievement, and with Ida B. Wells handed out The Reason Why the Colored American Is Not in the World's Columbian Exposition at the gates.

"The exhibit of the progress made by a race in 25 years of freedom as against 250 years of slavery, would have been the greatest tribute to the greatness and progressiveness of American institutions which could have been shown the world." Ida B. Wells  preface to The Reason Why, 1893
