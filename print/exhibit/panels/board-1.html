The resort · 1853 to 1856

Paul Cornell, a young Chicago lawyer, bought sixty lakefront acres of farm and tavern land in August 1853 and kept buying until he held about 300 acres between 51st and 55th Streets. He named the area Hyde Park to give it a refined image and sold it as an escape from a city that drained its sewage into the lake. In 1856 he gave the Illinois Central sixty acres for a station at 53rd Street and a guarantee of daily trains. The families he recruited were wealthy and white.

The South Park plan · 1869 to 1871

Cornell then led the campaign for public parks beside his own land, and the legislature passed the South Park act in 1869. The commissioners bought 1,055 acres of sandbar and marsh and hired Frederick Law Olmsted and Calvert Vaux, the designers of Central Park. Their 1871 plan showed lagoons, a canal down the Midway, and thirty miles of paths. The Great Fire burned the drawings that October. Jackson Park sat mostly unfinished for twenty years, and that open public land is why the World's Fair was placed there.

The World's Fair · 1893

Chicago built the World's Columbian Exposition in about two years, steel frames covered in white plaster. It drew about 27 million visits in a country of 65 million people. Black Americans were shut out of its planning, its exhibit halls, and nearly all of its jobs. At the gates, Ida B. Wells and Frederick Douglass handed out a pamphlet titled The Reason Why the Colored American Is Not in the World's Columbian Exposition.
