The Rules Change.
The Market Does Not.
1948–1968
Even after courts made racial covenants unenforceable, new rules kept Black families out and took wealth from the neighborhoods they built.
Blockbusting spread fear
Speculators told white homeowners that Black families moving in would "ruin" the block, then offered to buy their homes for less. The same operators resold those homes to Black buyers at nearly double the price, because banks would not lend across the color line.

The Black Belt
This narrow South Side corridor, graded red on the survey, held Black Chicago's homes, businesses, churches, and culture, built and sustained despite the barriers. Redlining shut off ordinary mortgages for nearly every block of it, and overcrowding followed.
Banking policy and "the wicked speculators exacted a fearful tribute from the Negro man and his family."
Attorney Mark Satter, who spent his career fighting contract sellers
Contract buying kept prices high
Speculators bought homes cheaply, then resold them using high-risk installment contracts. Buyers made big payments, but never built equity.

The buyers fight back
In the late 1960s, families formed the Contract Buyers League, confronted sellers as a group, and held payments in escrow through a strike. By 1971 they had forced 155 contracts open.
