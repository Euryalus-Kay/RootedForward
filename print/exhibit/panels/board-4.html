Urban renewal displaced us
1952
The university forms the South East Chicago Commission under attorney Julian Levi to steer what happens next.
1955
Demolition begins near 55th Street and Lake Park Avenue.
1958
The Hyde Park–Kenwood Urban Renewal Plan is approved, the first large federally supported plan in the U.S.
  • About 4,000 families displaced, most of them Black and low-income
  • $40 million in public money acquired land resold for about $4 million
  • Only 34 public housing units built
  • Of displaced white families, 46 in 100 stayed. Of displaced Black families, 17 in 100 did
What renewal built
The Obama Center
The Obama Presidential Center opened here on Juneteenth, June 19, 2026, on the ground where the 1893 fair stood.
An $850 million project built by Lakeside Alliance, a team led by four Black-owned Chicago firms. It promised jobs and investment. Who benefits, and who gets pushed out?
Harper Court
After renewal razed the 1891 artists' row, residents raised the money to build Harper Court in 1965, rents under $100, so the artisans could stay. In 2007 the university bought it for $6.8 million. A glass office tower stands there now.
Students pushed back · 1962
After paired Black and white testers proved the university was refusing Black tenants in its own buildings, students occupied the administration building for two weeks. The university changed the policy.
What remains
New investment has brought parks, libraries, and institutions. But displacement, higher costs, and unequal access are still here. Median rents near the Center are up about 43 percent since 2015, against 14 percent citywide, and the median East Woodlawn house has doubled since 2019 to $440,000.
"I want to stay. I don't want to be forced out." Kyana Butler, Woodlawn renter, 2026.
Walk this history
Walk Hyde Park is a free, student-researched audio tour of these blocks, thirteen stops and three detours over about four miles. Every number on these boards carries its source there.
What's next
Hyde Park's future depends on the choices we make now. We organize. We research. We push for policies that put people, not profit, at the center. Illinois now lets homeowners strike racist covenant language from their deeds. This is our neighborhood. We're building our future.
The story isn't over.
