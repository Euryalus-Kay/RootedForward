# Phase 3. Content architecture

## Owner's language ruling (August 28, 2026, supersedes the titles below)

The owner rejected every metaphorical title, deck, and caption in the
first draft. The standing rules, now also in project memory:

- Titles literally name the subject. The exhibition is **Who Could
  Live in Hyde Park**. The sheets are **Who Could Live in Hyde Park**
  (masthead), **The Rules That Kept Hyde Park White**, **Contract
  Sales and Urban Renewal**, **Hyde Park Today**. "The Ground Keeps
  Moving", "Manufactured Ground", "The Paperwork", and "The Walls
  Crack" are dead.
- People and institutions act; paper never does. No "paperwork
  decided", no "the ledger stays open".
- Captions state exactly what is pictured and when, nothing else.
- The running band carries "Hyde Park, Chicago" and the sheet number,
  not an exhibition wordmark.

Everything below this line predates the ruling and stands as process
record. Where a title or deck below conflicts with the ruling, the
panel sources in `panels/` are the truth.

## The thesis

The exhibition carries the same argument as the tour and the on-site
exhibit, in the owner's own words. For a century, ordinary paperwork
decided who could own a home in Hyde Park, and the money it moved
never came back. Every stage had identifiable authors, and many of
them worked within a mile of the ground the visitor is standing on.

The exhibition title is the one the owner already uses for this
material. **The Ground Keeps Moving.** The physical panels are the
traveling form of the same exhibit that lives at
rooted-forward.org/tours/chicago/hyde-park, so the digital and
physical programs share one name, one palette, and one fact registry.

## Why not sixteen stops

The tour is a route. The exhibition is an argument. Thirteen main
stops and three detours collapse into four eras, and every stop's
material is assigned to the era where it does the most work. Stops
that exist for wayfinding (the Del Prado, the Midway line, Rockefeller
Chapel, Stagg Field, Daley's) surrender their facts to the era panels
and do not appear as units. The pile under Stagg Field, the Ferris
wheel foundations, and the Robie House survive only where they serve
the housing story, or not at all.

## The four panels

Era ranges are set in the plat-sheet header of each panel. No colons
anywhere, per house rule.

### Panel 1 · MANUFACTURED GROUND · 1853 to 1893

The phrase is the tour's own ("Everything east of you now is
manufactured ground"). Purpose, orient the visitor and plant the
question. Hyde Park did not grow, it was assembled, and it was
assembled for a chosen customer. Lead visual, Charles Graham's
night-lit Grand Court, run large. Secondary, the 1871 Olmsted and
Vaux plan as a map object, the Hyde Park House, and the title page of
The Reason Why. The Wells quote is the panel's verbatim voice.

### Panel 2 · THE PAPERWORK · 1908 to 1942

The site exhibit's own chapter title. Purpose, show the instruments.
A club's pledge, a professional code, a deed covenant, a federal map.
Document-led panel, the only one where reading is the point. Lead
visual, the HOLC Chicago map redrawn from the government's files
(all 703 areas, Hyde Park called out yellow, the Black Belt red),
with the recorded covenant language and Article 34 set as typographic
documents. The bombings sit here with Binga's verbatim line. Davis
closes the panel as the human proof.

### Panel 3 · THE WALLS CRACK · 1937 to 1971

The site exhibit's chapter title for Hansberry, extended across what
came through the crack. Purpose, the cost. Hansberry v. Lee and
Shelley v. Kraemer open the era, then two systems run in parallel,
the contract-sale market (the color tax) and the clearance (the first
federally supported urban renewal program in the country). This is
the data panel. The ledger treatment carries the numbers, a paired
figure carries the return rates, and the era band carries the
chronology. Baldwin's verbatim line is the panel's voice. Lead
visual, the Russell Lee kitchenette photograph plus the ledger block.

### Panel 4 · THE GROUND TODAY · 1962 to 2026

Purpose, land the argument in the present and hand the visitor the
walk. Harper Court's four lives compress the whole story into one
corner. The Obama Center closes the 1893 loop on the same ground
where Wells and Douglass stood at the gates. The current pressure
(rents, Woodlawn ordinance, the Butler quote) keeps the panel honest
about the present tense. Route map, QR, and the covenant-modification
law give the visitor something to do. Lead visual, the opening-day
photograph of the museum tower.

### PANEL 1 · MANUFACTURED GROUND

EYEBROW · Hyde Park, Chicago · 1853 to 1893
DECK · Hyde Park did not grow. It was assembled, a resort staged on
filled land, and assembled for a chosen customer.

**A resort by design.** Paul Cornell, a young Chicago lawyer, bought
sixty lakefront acres from tavern keeper Electa Watson Garnsey's farm
in August 1853 and kept buying until he held about 300 acres between
51st and 55th Streets (T). He named the area Hyde Park for the
refined sound of it and marketed it as an escape from a city that
drained its sewage into the lake (T). In 1856 he gave the Illinois
Central sixty acres in exchange for a station at 53rd Street and
guaranteed daily trains (formation.ic_deal_60_acres). The families he
recruited were wealthy and white (T).

**The parks and the plan.** Cornell then led the campaign for public
parks beside his own land, and the legislature obliged in 1869 (T).
The commissioners bought 1,055 acres of sandbar and marsh and hired
Frederick Law Olmsted and Calvert Vaux, the designers of Central Park
(T). Their 1871 plan drew lagoons, a canal down the Midway, and
thirty miles of paths. The Great Fire burned the drawings that
October (T). Jackson Park sat mostly unfinished for twenty years,
which is exactly why the World's Fair landed on it (T).

**One gilded summer.** Chicago raised the World's Columbian
Exposition in about two years, steel frames dressed in white plaster
(T). It drew about 27 million visits in a country of 65 million
(fair.attendance_27m). Black Americans were shut out of its planning,
its exhibit halls, and nearly all of its jobs (T). At the gates, Ida
B. Wells and Frederick Douglass handed out a pamphlet titled The
Reason Why the Colored American Is Not in the World's Columbian
Exposition (T).

VOICE (verbatim, fair.wells_preface_1893) ·
"The exhibit of the progress made by a race in 25 years of freedom as
against 250 years of slavery, would have been the greatest tribute to
the greatness and progressiveness of American institutions which
could have been shown the world."
Ida B. Wells, preface to The Reason Why, 1893

CAPTIONS
- The Grand Court lit at night, drawn by Charles Graham, 1893. The
  White City stood for six months and burned within two. Library of
  Congress Prints and Photographs Division.
- Olmsted, Vaux & Co., plan for the South Park, 1871, north to the
  left. The canal down the Midway was never dug. Norman B. Leventhal
  Map & Education Center, Boston Public Library.
- The Hyde Park House, Cornell's resort centerpiece, on what was then
  the shore, 1870s. Public domain by age.
- Title page, The Reason Why, 1893. Wells handed out thousands of
  copies at the fair's gates.

LEDGER FOOT · 1833 Potawatomi land ceded under force · 1893 housing
stock built for a six-month fair

### PANEL 2 · THE PAPERWORK

EYEBROW · Hyde Park, Chicago · 1908 to 1942
DECK · Four kinds of paper decided who could live here. A club's
pledge, a professional code, a deed covenant, and a federal map. Each
had authors, and most of them worked within a mile of these blocks.

**The club, 1908.** In the autumn of 1908, attorney Francis Harper
organized the Hyde Park Improvement Protective Club, 350 members with
an openly stated goal of maintaining the color line
(colorline.club_founded, T). Members pledged to blacklist realtors
who sold to Black buyers. Black doctors, teachers, and business
owners had lived in Hyde Park for fifteen years. The club targeted
them anyway (T). Where paperwork failed, dynamite followed. White
Chicagoans set off 58 bombs at Black homes and at the offices of
agents who sold to Black families between July 1917 and March 1921
(bombings.total_58). No one was convicted (bombings.convictions_0).

VOICE (verbatim, T/CCRR) · "I will not run. The race is at stake and
not myself." Jesse Binga, banker, bombed repeatedly, to the riot
commission, 1920.

**The code, 1924.** DOCUMENT SET LARGE ·
"A Realtor should never be instrumental in introducing into a
neighborhood a character of property or occupancy, members of any
race or nationality, or any individuals whose presence will clearly
be detrimental to property values in that neighborhood."
Article 34, Code of Ethics, National Association of Real Estate
Boards, adopted June 6, 1924 (code.nareb_article34_1924). Enforced
from 310 South Michigan Avenue, seven miles from this ground (T).
Chicago's board had already voted, in 1921, to expel members who sold
to Black buyers on all-white blocks (code.cireb_expulsion_1921).

**The covenant, 1927.** The association's counsel, Nathan William
MacChesney, drafted a model racial covenant, and Chicago's real
estate board promoted it block by block (covenants.macchesney_template).
DOCUMENT · "No part of said premises shall in any manner be used or
occupied directly or indirectly by any negro or negroes" (T).
Hundreds of Cook County subdivisions carried covenants by the 1940s
(covenants.coverage_claim). The University of Chicago spent
$83,597.46 defending them in court between 1933 and 1947
(covenants.uchicago_defense_83597). The Hyde Park Herald admired the
result in 1928 as "like a marvelous delicately woven chain of armor" (T).

**The map, 1939 to 1940.** HOLC MAP HERO · Federal surveyors graded
every Chicago block for mortgage risk and colored a master map, green
best, blue still desirable, yellow declining, red hazardous
(redlining.holc_survey_chicago). The printed survey form recorded the
race of an area's residents before the condition of its houses
(walltext ch0). The Black Belt was graded red. Hyde Park was graded
yellow because Black neighborhoods bordered it (T). A red or yellow
grade could shut off ordinary mortgages for every house on a block (T).

**The proof, 1942.** The university gave William Allison Davis
tenure, the first for a Black scholar at a major white university
(T). No landlord in Hyde Park or Kenwood would rent to his family.
He commuted from across the color line his own employer had helped
defend in court (T).

LEDGER FOOT · 1908 a club to remove neighbors · 1919 dead in the
riot · 1927 covenants across the county · 1939 race scored as risk

### PANEL 3 · THE WALLS CRACK

EYEBROW · Hyde Park, Chicago · 1937 to 1971
DECK · The covenant wall cracked in this neighborhood in 1940 and
fell in 1948. What reached the families who came through it was a
market built to overcharge them, and then a plan that cleared the
blocks they had just bought.

**The crack.** In 1937 Carl Hansberry bought a brick three-flat at
6140 South Rhodes, inside a covenant the university had helped defend
(T). A mob gathered, and a chunk of concrete nearly struck his
daughter Lorraine, age seven (T). Hansberry v. Lee reached the
Supreme Court in 1940 and the covenant fell on its defective
signatures (cases.hansberry_1940, covenants.hansberry_frontage_54pct).
Shelley v. Kraemer ended court enforcement everywhere in 1948
(cases.shelley_1948). Lorraine Hansberry put the mob outside the
window into A Raisin in the Sun (T).

**The color tax.** Banks would not follow the ruling. Of Cook
County's 241 white-run savings and loans, exactly one would lend to a
Black family (contracts.lenders_241_1). Speculators filled the gap,
buying cheap from fleeing white owners and reselling to Black
families on installment contracts at an average markup of 84 percent
(contracts.markup_84pct). Miss one payment and the seller kept the
house and every dollar paid (T). Between 75 and 95 percent of homes
sold to Black Chicago families in the 1950s and 60s were sold on
contract (contracts.share_75_95). The take, in today's dollars, ran
between $3.2 and $4 billion (contracts.extraction_3_4b). In 1968 the
Contract Buyers League organized, struck, and forced hundreds of
contracts open (cbl.founded_1968, T).

**The clearance.** The university's answer to the changing
neighborhood was land clearance. Its South East Chicago Commission,
formed in 1952 under attorney Julian Levi, helped write an Illinois
law that let planners condemn blocks that only might become blighted
(renewal.secc_1952, T). Demolition began near 55th and Lake Park in
1955 (renewal.first_demolition_1955). On November 7, 1958, City
Council approved the Hyde Park-Kenwood plan, the first large
federally supported urban renewal program in the country
(renewal.approved_1958, T). Public agencies spent about $40 million
assembling and clearing the land and resold it to developers for
about $4 million (T). About 4,000 families were displaced
(renewal.families_displaced). The plan built 34 units of public
housing (T). Of displaced white families, 46 percent found homes in
Hyde Park-Kenwood. Of displaced Black families, 17 percent did
(renewal.white_return_46pct, renewal.black_return_17pct).

VOICE (verbatim, 1963 interview) · "urban renewal, which means moving
the Negroes out… it means Negro removal, that is what it means"
James Baldwin, 1963

CAPTIONS
- A Black Belt apartment house cut into one-room kitchenettes.
  Russell Lee, April 1941, Farm Security Administration. Library of
  Congress.
- I. M. Pei's University Apartments on their island in 55th Street,
  finished in 1961 on cleared land.

LEDGER FOOT · 1948 covenants ruled unenforceable · 1958 the color
tax, 84 percent over price · 1962 about 4,000 families removed ·
1970 hundreds of contracts forced open

### PANEL 4 · THE GROUND TODAY

EYEBROW · Hyde Park, Chicago · 1962 to 2026
DECK · Hyde Park stayed integrated when almost no American
neighborhood did. The bill went to the people with the least say,
and the ground is still moving.

**What the plan bought.** After renewal, Hyde Park held for decades
at roughly one-third Black and one-half white, one of the few
substantially integrated neighborhoods in the country (T). The
integration was real. So was the price, paid by the renters and
small businesses the plan removed (T). In 1962, after paired Black
and white testers proved the university was still refusing Black
tenants in its own buildings, students occupied the administration
building for two weeks. The university changed the policy (T).

**One corner, four lives.** In 1891 Frederick Gibbs built twenty-six
wooden shops at 57th Street for fair visitors. Artists rented them
for fifty years. The Land Clearance Commission bought and razed the
row in 1962, and residents raised money to build Harper Court in
1965, rents under one hundred dollars, so the artisans could stay
(T). In 2007 the university bought Harper Court for $6.8 million and
replaced it with the glass tower standing there now (T).

**The Center on the fairgrounds.** The Obama Presidential Center
opened on Juneteenth, June 19, 2026, on the ground where the 1893
fair stood (T). Lakeside Alliance, a construction team led by four
Black-owned Chicago firms, built it, with a goal of placing as much
as half of more than $300 million in subcontracts with diverse
businesses (T). Lines from the 2015 Selma speech are carved where
Wells and Douglass once handed out their pamphlet at the gates (T).

**The pressure now.** Since the Center was announced, median rents
nearby have risen about 43 percent, against roughly 14 percent
citywide (T). The median East Woodlawn house doubled from 2019 to
2025, to $440,000 (T). The 2020 Woodlawn Housing Preservation
Ordinance reserved 52 city lots for affordable housing. Five years
on, one 58-unit building had opened and about $2 million sat unspent
(T).

VOICE (verbatim, T/Hyde Park Herald 2026) · "I want to stay. I don't
want to be forced out." Kyana Butler, Woodlawn renter, 2026.

**Walk the evidence.** The free Walk Hyde Park audio tour visits the
ground on this wall in thirteen stops and three detours, about four
miles in chronological order, researched and written by students (T).
Illinois now lets homeowners file to strike racist covenant language
from their deeds (redlining.illinois_removal_2022). Policies made by
people can be changed by people (T, the tour's own closing claim).

opens covenant removal · 2026 the Center opens on the fairground ·

## Banner condensation rule

Banners carry the same argument at 175 to 325 words. Cuts, in order,
per panel. P1 drops the parks section into one sentence held by the
plan image. P2 drops the Herald line and compresses the club and the
proof. P3 drops the Pei caption and the CBL sentence, keeps every
number. P4 drops the sit-in and the ordinance detail, keeps Butler,
Harper Court, the Center, the map and the code.

## What was deliberately left out

The nuclear pile, the Ferris wheel, Robie architecture for its own
sake, hotel genealogy, the Klan at the Sisson (one line was
considered and cut for space; it lives in the tour), Daley's, the
1919 riot death toll (the bombing campaign carries that era on the
panel), Nichols Park, the Midway ethnological displays (the fair
panel keeps its exclusion argument tight; the tour carries the
full account). The panels send people to the tour for all of it.
